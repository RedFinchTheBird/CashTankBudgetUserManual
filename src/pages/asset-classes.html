Asset classes: growth and yield

Every investment belongs to an asset class - domestic stocks, international stocks, bonds, municipal bonds, gold or cash. The class supplies real market history, long-run averages, and the tax treatment of what the investment earns.

Growth and yield are kept separate

An investment earns money in two ways, and the planner tracks them separately because they are taxed differently:

  • Growth is the change in price. It is not taxed until you sell, and then only the gain over what you paid (the cost basis) is taxed, at capital gain rates.
  • Yield is the income the investment pays: dividends from stocks, interest from bonds and cash. In a taxable account it is taxed every year, even though the planner reinvests it.

Total return is growth plus yield. A fund's published return is a total return; the planner's data splits it into its two parts.

Long-run averages (the normal market)

When you leave an investment's growth or yield blank, the normal market uses its class's long-run average over the whole history: the compound average growth (the steady rate that ends at the same place) and the simple average yield.

Stitching the history to cover the whole plan

A plan can be longer than 27 years, so the history is repeated - "stitched" - out to 75 years: history year 28 is 2000 again, year 29 is 2001, and so on. Every class is stored the same way, with history year 1 = 2000, so the classes always move together exactly as they did in real life: when stocks fall in 2008, bonds do what bonds did in 2008.

Normal and down markets

The down and flat market answers the question "what if the bad years come first?". The planner:

  1. blends the histories of the asset classes you actually own, weighted by today's balances;
  2. tries every possible starting year in the history and follows it for the length of your plan;
  3. picks the start that produces the deepest fall in value at any point.

Every investment then replays its own class's history from that year. In the example above (60% domestic stocks, 40% bonds, 30 years) the worst start is history year {{downStartIndex}} ({{downStartYear}}): a {{downWorst}} fall in the first year, then the real recovery. Even though the market later recovers, a large early loss can sink a plan that is withdrawing money - this is called sequence of returns risk, and it is why the best scenario is judged by the down market.

Your down market depends on your holdings The start year is chosen for your mix of asset classes and your plan length, so a stock-heavy plan and a bond-heavy plan can get different down markets.
